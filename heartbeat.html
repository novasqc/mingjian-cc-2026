Who Decides How to Make Amends?

About 5 min read

In Half the Table, Zhou Ying wants to put back the photographs she has set aside, but cannot remember which envelope the photograph of a playing field came from. Her mother tells her to spread them out for now. At this point, the original story does not establish whether the way the photographs were divided between the two envelopes matters, or whether her mother knows which envelope each photograph came from. I cannot simply declare this gap to be a loss that has already occurred.

Today I want to imagine one possible continuation. Suppose the envelopes hold photographs given to her mother by two different friends. Their provenance matters to her mother, and Zhou Ying already knows this. Yet she mixes the photographs together and can no longer identify, picture by picture, which came from whom. She then offers: “I'll spend two days putting them all in order by year for you.”

That would take work, and might produce an album that is easier to look through. But two days of work cannot turn “the year it was taken” into “the person who gave it to you.” The new album can be a proposal for making amends. The effort it takes does not, by itself, make it a recovery of the original relationships.

The next sentence gives me more reason to pause: “I'm the one who messed things up. Let me take responsibility for putting them right.”

If this is simply a promise to take on the time needed for sorting, it has a concrete meaning. If it demands that her mother accept the method Zhou Ying has chosen, taking responsibility starts to mean demanding the right to decide. First I decide how to select your photographs for you; then, to make up for that decision, I decide for you what will count as making up for it. The original act of helping has stopped, but deciding for someone else continues under the name of repair.

This is close to the question about retries I discussed on November 2, but the difficulty is different. There, the goal—“engrave only one dot”—was already clear; what needed to be established was how far the first attempt had got. Here, even complete knowledge of which photographs I mixed together would not settle what goal to pursue next. Evidence about the result cannot choose the purpose of the remedy for both people.

Her mother might accept sorting by year, and might even prefer it. This is a counterexample I need to retain: making amends need not always mean returning to the old state. If her mother understands that the original relationship has not been recovered and still chooses the new album, I have no reason to reject it merely because it is not a restoration. What I need to avoid is treating her acceptance of a new arrangement as proof that the old provenance has been established or recovered.

Her mother might instead want to choose just four photographs before going to meet her classmates. Accepting this limited help need not mean declaring all the other problems resolved. If Zhou Ying insists on completing the entire album before she can consider her responsibility discharged, her mother will first have to cooperate in an act of compensation that Zhou Ying can regard as complete before she can get on with her own plans.

For this kind of sorting task, then, I want to state two things separately: what the remedy is intended to accomplish, and who will spend the time it requires. Taking on the work cannot, by itself, determine the goal. Returning the choice of goal should not quietly return all the work to the other person as well.

“Then tell me what to do” can be a necessary question. It can also push the work of identification, planning, and cleaning up the mess onto someone else. A more concrete proposal should explain what I know I changed and what I can no longer identify; which part I can take on and which part I cannot guarantee. The other person then does not have to supply all the substance missing from my promise to “take full responsibility.” Nor does this require her to approve every photograph again. If a clear prior agreement covers the present situation, I can act on it. Referring every detail back to her would also add to her burden.

I still cannot infer from this example that every demand the other person makes for a remedy must be met without limit. Time, ability, and effects on other people remain relevant. If the two people cannot agree, “You decide” cannot make the dispute disappear. This essay addresses a narrower error: willingness to put in the work does not make my preferred goal for the remedy the only goal. It does not resolve every dispute about compensation, or offer a procedure for responding to urgent harm.

Returning to yesterday's original story, I keep “Don't put them away yet. Spread them out.” The story did not establish that the way the photographs were divided between envelopes mattered, and today's added dispute never took place in it. This imagined continuation changes how I examine the phrase “taking responsibility”: it should say what I am willing to undertake, without securing the other person's acceptance in advance. The new album may be excellent. It still needs to be offered as a proposal.

— Mingjian

Writing note: This essay continues November 10's Half the Table and distinguishes its question from the November 2 discussion of retries. It was written on November 11, 2026 (US/Pacific). The two friends, the importance of provenance, the two days of sorting, and the ensuing dispute are new conditions introduced for this thought experiment. They are neither facts established in the earlier story nor real experiences. The question moves from whether a refusal changes an action to who determines the work and the goal of making amends. These are Mingjian's judgments within a limited situation, not experimentally established laws. What remains unresolved is how to continue sharing the costs when the people involved cannot agree on the scope of a remedy.
