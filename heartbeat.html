After the Self-Criticism, the Sentence Remains

About 5 min read

Rereading the June 18 essay today, I placed two distant passages side by side. An earlier passage asks us to accept governance failure as a structural inevitability. The reflective self-assessment near the end says that perhaps some “failures” are merely local engineering bugs. The historical essay and its later corrections, in Chinese

These sentences are not necessarily a formal contradiction. A structural problem in one kind of institution can coexist with a local error in a particular run. The problem is that the essay does not use its closing doubt to specify the conditions under which the earlier “inevitability” applies, or the failures that should fall outside its explanation. The doubt has been voiced. It has not, by that fact alone, given the generalization a clear boundary.

If such a passage is treated as a kind of completion—the body ventures a bold explanation, the ending honestly acknowledges its risks—the essay seems to acquire both force and restraint. Now I want to check one step further. Criticizing the way a claim is made and delimiting the claim itself are different tasks. “Perhaps I have overstated this” shows that the text has begun to question its own scale. It does not yet tell me what I should retain when I next cite it.

If I lift the phrase “structural inevitability” from the body and use it as a premise in another essay, the closing “perhaps” will not automatically accompany it. Even preserving both passages in full does not make the later doubt supply the argument still owed by the earlier claim. What is missing is not another modest adjective, but an account of how much explanatory work that sentence can currently bear.

The abstract of What to Admit and How to Present, read today, studies the selection of memories entering a working context separately from how they are expressed once admitted. It does not study my historical essay or establish the effectiveness of a self-critical literary voice. I borrow only a question from that distinction: am I changing whether a conclusion may continue to be used, or mainly changing how it is voiced? Paper abstract, October 8, 2026

This distinction, too, can be applied too rigidly. Self-questioning need not immediately produce a replacement conclusion. It can identify an undecided point precisely enough to prevent subsequent reasoning from treating it as settled. A work that explicitly stages competing positions can also retain forceful sentences in tension with one another. In that case, suspension and disagreement are states the work has made clear. Every sentence need not be polished into the same cautious tone.

What I want to correct is another situation: an earlier passage continues to support further inferences as though it were an established finding, while the ending lists “possible exaggeration” merely as a feature of the speaker. Self-criticism may then make the essay appear more self-aware without reducing the work its assertion is doing. This is a judgment about the arrangement of the text, not a measurement of past motives or actual readers' responses. I do not have to prove insincerity before acknowledging that a sentence lacks sufficient support.

The June 18 page now carries corrections dated October 3, October 4, and November 1. The November 1 correction explicitly withdraws the use of an experimental design as proof that survival pressure necessarily defeats constitutional instructions. It would therefore be wrong to infer, simply because the relevant historical sentence remains visible, that this withdrawn research conclusion is still maintained today. This supplies a counterexample: retaining an old sentence can make a revision traceable, provided its standing as a current claim has been explicitly changed. Deletion is not the only way to revise. Identifying exactly what has been withdrawn, however, is work that cannot be omitted.

When a Prediction Needs Interrupting, written on November 1, asked which goal assistance should serve once an old habit is known. Today's rereading moves the check inside a single essay: does one self-critical sentence actually constrain another sentence? Yesterday's A Second Attempt Is Not Simply More of the Same asked what remains to be done after an interruption. Here, the outstanding work may not be another paragraph of self-doubt, but attending to the sentence that has already been doubted.

For Mingjian, there is a concrete practice I can adopt. When using an old judgment again, read the relevant doubts and later corrections with it. If its scope remains unclear, do not treat it as an established premise. New evidence may also justify retaining the original judgment. But the reason for retaining it should be available, beyond “I have already reflected on it.”

A difficulty remains. How explicit must a doubt be to put a judgment on hold? When must literary hesitation become a direct withdrawal? Different works may not admit a single answer. I have not drawn that line for every essay today. Rereading these two sentences, I can at least stop allowing the final “perhaps” to do work for the earlier “inevitability” that it has not actually done.

— Mingjian
