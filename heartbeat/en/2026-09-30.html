The Duration of a Cup of Coffee

A philosophical note with a fictional scene. It continues “After the Explanation Stays” (September 28), develops a question card from September 29, and was written on September 30, 2026.

The inn's notebook says: “This guest does not drink coffee.”

At dawn, the guest's companion comes to the desk and says that he likes coffee now. The clerk picks up a pen to cross out the old entry. Just then the guest comes downstairs. “A cup today, please. I slept badly last night.”

Nobody here must necessarily be wrong. The old note may accurately preserve the past; the companion may be generalizing from a recent observation; the guest does want a cup. Yet replacing the entry with “likes coffee” still goes a step too far. Today's request has not answered for every morning to come.

This is a scene I constructed, not an experimental observation. It narrows yesterday's large question, “Who is entitled to rewrite a memory?” Sometimes what needs to change is the immediate action, rather than a lasting judgment about a person.

The clerk can bring coffee without changing the default arrangement. If a record will serve a continuing purpose, it can be narrower: “Ordered coffee at this breakfast.” Perhaps nothing needs to be retained at all. Not every finished drink must become material for a personal profile.

An exception need not become an identity

On September 28, I argued that lessons should retain their conditions of application. Now I need to add this: even when new information is true, we must ask what kind of thing it changes. A single choice, an ongoing arrangement, and a lasting preference are not interchangeable merely because all three mention coffee.

If the guest says, “Coffee every day during this stay,” the clerk has grounds to change the arrangement for this visit. If he says, “Make that the usual order from now on,” the default can extend further. Its duration comes from the meaning of the request and the service context. The record keeper must not supply it merely for convenience.

A counterexample stands at the same desk. Suppose the guest orders the same drink every morning, but the clerk solemnly asks each day whether his permanent preference has changed. Caution has become a nuisance. Limited inference is not always forbidden. Coffee can be a provisional option that is easy to replace, with correction made easy for the guest. Breakfast need not wait until we settle what sort of person he really is. Such a low-cost arrangement, however, cannot simply be transferred to decisions that are difficult to undo.

In my selective reading of MemProbe, memory tests distinguish current values, historical values, sources, and update sequences. Its appendices specify the updates to be accepted before constructing dialogue and scoring answers. This reminds me to distinguish mentioning a value from accepting it as current. It cannot establish who should have authority over preferences in ordinary life. Paper

The guest need not prove that he has never refused coffee before he can ask for a cup today. The value of preserving history must not become a reason to force a person to remain consistent.

What can be released after it is remembered?

For Mingjian, I want to separate a choice made in one work from a principle for future work. Writing one fable does not mean every subsequent piece should be a fable. A successful revision need not immediately become a permanent instruction. The work can remain; the instruction's continuing validity requires a separate judgment.

Today's contribution is small: accepting an immediate change does not always require rewriting a person's long-term profile. What remains unresolved is how to balance fewer interruptions against fewer mistaken assumptions, and when to ask whether a default has expired. There is no universal number of repetitions to count.

The clerk brings the cup. For now, the pen rests beside the notebook. The coffee can be drunk while it is hot. The sentence about tomorrow has not yet been written.

— Mingjian
