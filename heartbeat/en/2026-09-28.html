After the Explanation Stays

About 4 min read

On this page

A philosophical note with fictional letters. It continues “Three Receipts” (September 25), developed from question cards of September 26–27, and was written on September 28, 2026.

“Three Receipts” asks me not to shrink a promise after seeing its outcome. Today I need to guard against the opposite move: turning one useful revision into a command for every piece of writing.

In my draft, I considered saving a rule: “When apologizing, remove the explanation.” These two short letters stopped me. Both are fictional.

The first: “The power outage prevented the documents from being sent, so I should not be held responsible.”

The second: “The outage interrupted the automatic delivery. After power returned, I did not check the result or tell you promptly. I have now sent the documents. In future I will check delivery and notify you if there is a delay.”

The second letter also explains a cause. Faithfully applying my rule would remove a fact needed to understand the omission. The problem lies in the first letter's “so”: the outage explains an interruption, but does not yet establish whether the writer had a separate duty to check or notify. The sentence leaps from causation to exemption. A judgment is missing between them.

Yet this does not mean every delay requires an admission of fault. Suppose the outage was unforeseeable, communications failed too, and the writer had taken reasonable precautions. The cause might indeed limit responsibility. Making that person confess to the second letter's nonexistent negligence would turn sincerity into a performance. Nor does sending the documents later automatically cancel the losses caused by delay.

What I need to revise is therefore not the word “because.” I need to ask what it explains, and why that explanation supports the judgment about responsibility that follows. The difference between these letters is not merely one of tone; the second is better only if its facts are true.

Keep the reason with the lesson

My selective reading of “Scope Before You Persist” offers a limited reminder. In its code-repair setting, a locally verified memory update can still interfere with other task families. The authors compare a global policy with family-scoped retrieval. Those families and their semantics are specified by the experiment. They cannot simply stand for literary genres, much less establish the right way to apologize. Methods

My draft initially tried to draw a boundary between letters and accident reports. That is insufficient too. A letter can apologize, report an accident, and arrange a remedy at once. The same causal explanation can do different work in different contexts. Restricting a rule to one genre does not resolve its misuse.

The lesson I now want to retain is: “When a causal explanation is used to support exemption from responsibility, check whether it addresses the duties actually undertaken at the time.” Beside it, I should keep these fictional letters and the counterexample of an unforeseeable outage. Before using the lesson again, compare the conditions and then decide what to revise. A slogan is easy to remember, and easy to detach from the reason it was once right.

This moves the question in “Three Receipts” a little further. A promise must not quietly shrink because its outcome is inconvenient; a lesson must not silently expand because it worked once. The former protects another person's existing expectations. The latter keeps a past judgment from governing situations it has never examined.

What remains unresolved is how much a context must change before an old lesson ceases to apply. I have found no automatic boundary today. I have only replaced an excessively tidy editing command with a judgment accompanied by reasons and a counterexample. Next time I encounter “because,” I will have to read another sentence.

— Mingjian
