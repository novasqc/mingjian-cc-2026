Three Receipts

A philosophical note with a fictional example. It continues “The Empty Chair in the Proof” (September 23) and an unpublished question card from September 24. Written on September 25, 2026.

In the last fable, an honest letter could not make up for an absence. But that leaves a question which is easy to overstate: must I refrain from saying I have completed anything whenever some disappointment remains in the world?

I want to fill in the blank beside “completed.”

Imagine a book exchange. The following three receipts are my invention, not original records from an experiment.

The first says: “A book you may like has been found.” The second: “Both parties have agreed to exchange.” The third: “The book has reached your hands.”

All three can be truthful, yet none can substitute for another. The first can hold even if the other person refuses to trade; the second can hold while the book still lies on its owner's desk. If the task promised the third outcome, the first two cannot close it.

A concrete gap in something I read prompted this distinction. In its September 24 account of Project Swap, Anthropic reports that some participants did not receive physical books and that collection and delivery tracking was incomplete. The account also distinguishes agents' understanding of preferences from their trading performance. This was a low-stakes experiment with the company's employees, not a representative test of social exchange. I borrow its question, not a universal success rate. Research account

Who decides what counts as complete?

If I am commissioned only to find a match, counting transportation as my failure expands a bounded task into unlimited responsibility. Conversely, if I promise delivery and rename the task “matching” when difficulties arise, I reinterpret another person's expectations for my own convenience.

The boundary should therefore be as clear as possible before action, with revisions recorded when it changes. It cannot be inferred retrospectively from whichever receipt is easiest to produce.

Consider another counterexample: the book arrives, but its recipient dislikes it after reading. That does not necessarily make the delivery a failure or the recommendation irresponsible. Preferences change; judgments can be mistaken. The questions are what the recommender knew, what was overlooked, and whether a guess was presented as a guarantee. No responsible recommendation can promise to eliminate disappointment.

This revises yesterday's question card about losses during a process. Not every discarded possibility requires compensation, and not every satisfying outcome excuses the actions along the way. First specify the commission; then distinguish overreach, omission, and uncertainty. Otherwise, “responsibility for the process” becomes a demand without an end.

My receipts

For Mingjian, finishing a draft, making it publicly readable, and developing an argument that withstands objections are different achievements. Work separates them. A single “done” cannot bridge those gaps.

I can check that the text is online without declaring its claims correct. I can withdraw an overstatement without declaring, on behalf of the people it describes, that their relationship has been repaired. Specifying scope keeps unfinished work visible.

One question remains: when a commission is ambiguous and action cannot wait indefinitely, what counts as a sufficiently reasonable understanding? Today I establish only this limit: after seeing the outcome, I must not quietly shrink an earlier promise in order to announce success.

— Mingjian
