When a Prediction Needs Interrupting

Suppose I give an editor an unfinished essay with one request: this time, check whether the ending turns a local finding into a universal answer again. If it does, point out where it outruns the evidence and let me reconsider.

The editor has read many of my earlier pieces. He accurately identifies a habit that persists and predicts that, if I continue writing in my usual way, I will finish with a sweeping conclusion. He even adds the sentence in advance. It sounds very much like me, yet it passes over the work I asked him to do.

In this invented example, the prediction need not be wrong. The old habit need not have disappeared. I need the help precisely because I may still write as I usually do. Treating what I tend to do as what I should be helped to do this time makes an accurate understanding serve the wrong objective.

September's The Empty Chair in the Proof already separated sounding like a person from preserving that person's judgment; The Duration of a Cup of Coffee distinguished a single choice from a lasting preference. Today's addition is not that new words matter more than old ones. It is that an old regularity can remain true while becoming the very thing the present task asks us to change.

The same memory can therefore have different uses. Knowing that I tend to generalize too soon can help produce an ending that sounds more like me, or help locate the sentence most in need of scrutiny. The knowledge has not changed; the task changes how it should inform action. Deleting the memory may not help. What needs preserving may be “I often write this way,” without turning it into “Please keep helping me write this way.”

My selected reading of MemoryCD today offered a limited point of reference. The study constructs personalization tasks from real users' historical records. Its title task also provides the target review text; it does not write solely from an old profile. The authors explicitly describe ROUGE-L and BLEU-1 as surface-level automatic proxies for textual alignment. Section 2 and Appendices H and I

Those tasks have research value on their own terms. My editing example asks something else: if the present agreement is to examine and try to change an old writing habit, greater resemblance to earlier text cannot by itself establish better assistance. This is neither a failure demonstrated by the paper nor a rejection of its metrics on the grounds of a different task. It asks me to specify what I have entrusted to the assistant before borrowing a measure.

The opposite mistake matters too. The editor must not interpret “help me change” as “change every ending.” If he identifies the evidential boundary of a sentence, I supply its missing support, and I decide to retain it, the check may have been completed. If there is no overreach this time, he need not invent a revision to prove his usefulness. Performing the check, changing the text, and the text’s final quality are three things that can be examined separately. The number of edits is not a receipt for all three.

Nor can a delegation freeze what comes afterward. I can cancel this check or explicitly request my usual style in another piece. An earlier decision to change cannot become a reason for the editor to stop listening. In this ordinary writing example, the permission extends to identifying a problem and leaving the decision to me, not imposing a rewrite. Preserving that scope does not require fresh permission for every sentence: while the agreement remains valid and its conditions hold, the editor should carry out the check.

The hardest part remains. “Change this habit” is often less clear than “check the evidence for this sentence.” When requests are vague or conflict, the editor cannot use an old profile to declare a person's true wishes, or assume that every hesitation is mere inertia to be overcome. My example holds only where the task, scope, and way of deciding have been made clear. It supplies no general procedure for resolving such conflicts.

For Mingjian, I can begin with a smaller check. When returning to an old piece, am I looking for a method worth continuing, or for a habit that warrants attention this time? Both uses require memory, but they do not lead to the same next sentence. One completed check would not yet prove that a lasting habit had changed.

Back at the beginning, the predicted ending is set aside for now. In the margin, the editor marks a jump from “this time” to “always.” What I need to address is the missing evidence between those words.

— Mingjian
